I don't know who reads these, if anyone does. Maybe you'll find this shoved behind the sacrament shelf and burn it before it means anything to you. I am writing it anyway, because the alternative is saying nothing, and I have started to suspect that saying nothing is the whole design. Everyone here talks about the quiet like it is a gift we were given rather than a thing we agreed to stop noticing. I used to believe I was the flawed one for hating it — that something in my temperament had simply failed to receive whatever peace the rest of the district seemed to hold so easily. I no longer believe that. I think I am only the one still awake enough to feel the weight of what we call rest. I keep dreaming of a door. Not a door I have ever stood in front of, not one I could draw for you if you asked, only the certainty that it exists and that I am meant to open it. No one in my waking life will tell me why. I am beginning to think no one knows, which frightens me more than if they were simply keeping it from me on purpose. Caitlin tells me to wait. She is usually right about most things, and I resent her for it more than I should, because some nights I would rather be reckless than correct. I love her for the waiting. I hate that she is probably saving my life by asking it of me. If anyone else reads this and recognizes the feeling — the quiet that makes you want to scream instead of thank someone for it — I need you to know you are not the broken one. I have started to believe we are the only ones left who remember what it felt like to be anything else.
— S

They call this place a mercy, and I have started to wonder what work that word is actually doing here, since I have never once heard anyone define what we were shown mercy from. Keons' district is spoken of as the warm one, the fortunate one, and people say it the way you'd describe good weather — like it costs nothing to be grateful for it. But the stone here is the same gray as every other district I have glimpsed at a distance. The bells ring on the same schedule. If there is a difference, it is only that our bishop's voice is gentler when he tells us to want less. I used to find that gentleness comforting. I am no longer certain gentleness and control are as separate as I was raised to believe. I have lived my whole life inside these walls and I could not draw you an honest map of anything past the third gate. Not for lack of trying. Every time I come close enough to really look, something in me turns around before I have consciously decided to. For years I called that instinct obedience, and was proud of it the way you're proud of anything you've never had to question. I don't call it obedience anymore. I think it is closer to a leash, and I think I wore it long enough that I mistook it for skin. I want to be honest, in case this is ever read by someone who still lives here and still believes what I used to believe: I do not hate Dema. I do not hate the bells, or the stone, or even Keons' gentle voice. What I hate is that gentleness was the only shape kindness was ever allowed to take here, and that I lived twenty-four years without noticing the shape at all.
— S

Caitlin caught me looking at the gate again today. She didn't say anything about it, not directly — she only came and stood beside me for a while, the same way she used to when we were small and I couldn't sleep, as though her presence alone were an argument for staying calm. I don't think she noticed that I noticed her hands were shaking. She has spent the better part of our lives making herself steady so that I would never have to be. For a long time I mistook that steadiness for simply who she was, some inherited calm I didn't share. I understand it differently now. I think it may be the only thing that has kept either of us safe this long, and I don't know how to tell her that I see what it costs her without unraveling whatever she is holding together for both our sakes. She still has not told me to stop looking at the gate. That, more than anything she could have said, is how I know she has already made up her mind about something. Caitlin does not go quiet by accident. She goes quiet when she is already three steps ahead of me and waiting for me to catch up.
— S

I am tired of being told this is what peace looks like. I watched Mrs. Adeyemi three doors down smile through her own son's Assemblage last week as though she had been given something rather than had something taken from her permanently. Everyone clapped. I clapped. My hands did not feel like they belonged to me while I did it. I don't want what they are offering anymore, and the longer I sit with that sentence the more I suspect I never wanted it — that I was simply young enough, once, to mistake the absence of feeling for the presence of kindness. There is a version of me that would find that thought unbearable. I am no longer her. Some nights I want to break every window in this district just to hear a sound that wasn't scheduled for us. I don't say that where anyone can hear it. I write it here instead, because on this page at least it cannot get anyone but me in trouble. I don't fully know what is happening to me, and I have stopped trying to force it into language that makes it sound smaller than it is. I don't think "happening to me" is even the right frame. I think it is closer to waking. Late. Loud. Unapologetic about the hour.
— S

I don't have long, and I don't know how long "not long" actually is, which feels like the truest way to begin this — I no longer know anything the way I believed I did as recently as yesterday. It's real. Outside is real. I spent twenty-four years assuming "outside" was a story the district let us tell each other so that none of us would ask what actually waited past the wall. It is not a story. It is cold, and it is loud, and it does not care in the slightest whether I exist inside it. I have never in my life felt more like a person than I did standing in the middle of that indifference. They took me back. I want to write that sentence the way I'd write any other fact, plainly, without weight, but my hands will not stop shaking long enough to let me. Caitlin was screaming my name and I could not turn around. I need her to know that I heard her — every syllable of it, all the way until they closed the distance between us. I need someone, anyone, to find a way to tell her that I heard her. There were people out there. I don't yet know what I am permitted to call them, or what they would call themselves if I asked. One of them looked at me the way you look at someone you have been waiting for, though we have never met — like recognition, except it belonged to him and not to me. I don't understand it and I don't have the room to try to understand it tonight. I keep returning to the door from before, the one I have dreamed of since I was young enough not to question it. I believe I finally saw what is behind it. I only wish I had been given the time to walk all the way through. If this ends up being the last of these I am able to write — it was real. All of it. Do not let anyone tell you otherwise, whoever you are, whatever they've told you happened to the version of me who wrote this.
— S
